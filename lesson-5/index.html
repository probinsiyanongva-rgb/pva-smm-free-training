A content calendar is an operating document. It should help the client and VA understand what is planned, why it exists, what is needed, who must approve it, and what stage it is in.

5.3 Plan backwards from the publish date

Every post has a publish date, but the work behind it has earlier deadlines. Work backwards: when must the asset be ready, when must the caption go to the client, and how long does approval usually take? If the client usually needs three days to review, a post due to publish on Friday must reach her by Tuesday at the latest. That gap is the approval buffer. Give every post an owner (who does the next step) and a due date (when that step must be done), so nothing waits without anyone noticing.

5.4 When the schedule changes

Plans change: a product arrives late, the client moves a launch, or an asset isn't ready. When that happens, update the calendar first, then move the due dates that depend on it, and tell the client what has moved and why. Don't fill the gap by quietly publishing something that hasn't been approved. Note the change in the calendar so anyone checking it later can see what happened.

Observe — calendar overload

28 posts, but only 12 can realistically get approved

A beginner plans 28 posts in one month even though the client can realistically approve 12. Explain why this is a workflow problem, not evidence of ambition.

More posts than the client can approve doesn't mean more gets published — it means a bottleneck. Drafts pile up waiting for review, the VA spends time creating content that may never go out, and the client feels rushed or overwhelmed instead of in control. A calendar sized to real approval capacity (here, 12) is more useful than a full one that mostly sits in "Awaiting Approval." Ambition should show up in the quality and variety of 12 posts, not in producing volume nobody can process.
